4. Temporal Topology Screening Analysis

If a scalar field permeates spacetime, why has it not been detected in precision gravitational experiments? This section answers that question. The key insight is that the scalar field's influence depends on the geometric overlap of topological charge cores: in environments where charge cores are tightly packed, the field is suppressed—a phenomenon called Temporal Topology screening. Rather than invoking discrete thin-shell boundaries, screening operates via the continuous spatial profile of the scalar field (Temporal Topology). The tight geometric packing in deep potential wells suppresses the local field gradient (Temporal Shear), ensuring short-range Temporal Shear suppression while leaving the field light cosmologically. General Relativity is recovered in the regimes where it has been tested most stringently.

The screening factor $S$ quantifies this suppression. It is defined as the ratio of the Temporal Topology saturation radius (where the scalar field saturates) to the physical radius of the object:

\begin{equation} \label{eq:screening_factor} S = \frac{R_T}{R_{phys}}. \end{equation}

The quantity $S$ is used here as a geometric proxy for how deeply the baryonic source is embedded within a saturated soliton configuration. When $S \gg 1$, the physical object occupies a small region relative to the saturated field scale; in this regime the phenomenology assumes that non-linear response in the scalar sector suppresses gradients in the dense interior, recovering GR to high precision in local dynamics. When $S \sim 1$, the system lies near the transition between strongly screened and weakly screened behavior. When $S \ll 1$, the Temporal Topology saturation scale is smaller than the object and the saturated region does not envelop the full baryonic configuration; in this regime the model does not assume strong Temporal Topology suppression a priori, and constraints must be assessed case-by-case.

\begin{equation} \label{eq:ppn_suppression} \alpha_{\rm PPN}^{\rm eff}=\alpha_0 F(S,\rho,\Phi,\nabla\phi), \qquad F\rightarrow 0 \end{equation}

in the screened or source-charge-suppressed limit. The geometric factor $S$ is not itself the observable PPN coupling. Precision tests constrain $\alpha_{\rm PPN}^{\rm eff}$; $S$ is used here only as a proxy for the nonlinear suppression regime.

The near-transition regime $S \sim 0.4$–$0.7$ is resolved by the corpus's channel-projection structure (Paper 0). The screening response is not a single function of $S$: clock-comparison and transport observables probe the amplitude projection $S_A$, while orbital and geodesic observables probe the source-charge/shear projection $\mathcal S_\Sigma$, which is suppressed more strongly for the pairwise configurations used in ephemeris tests. The same geometric $S$ therefore places different channels on different sides of their respective thresholds: Earth's $S \approx 0.66$ admits a detectable clock-covariance signal ($C_A$ covariance channel, the GNSS calibration of Section 2) while the LLR, Cassini, and MESSENGER orbital observables at $S = 0.42$–$0.65$ remain GR-consistent through the $\mathcal S_\Sigma$ projection. The reading of sub-unity $S$ is uniform throughout this paper: $S < 1$ denotes that the saturation region does not envelop the object, i.e. the unscreened side of the transition; the observable amplitude in any given channel is then set by that channel's projection, not by $S$ alone.

4.1 The White Dwarf Stress Test

White Dwarfs are the ideal stress test for two reasons. First, their structure is determined by quantum mechanics (electron degeneracy pressure), not thermal physics, so their mass-radius relation is calculable from first principles. Second, their mass-radius scaling runs in the opposite direction to the saturation scaling (see Figure 4):

  • White Dwarf (low-mass non-relativistic limit): $R_{\rm WD} \propto M^{-1/3}$ — heavier stars are smaller (steepening toward $M_{\rm Ch} = 1.44\,M_\odot$ under relativistic degeneracy; Nauenberg 1972)
  • Saturation Scale (TEP): $R_T \propto M^{+1/3}$ — heavier sources have larger saturation radii

These opposing scalings create a "scissors" pattern: as mass increases, the geometric saturation radius $R_T(M)$ grows while the physical radius $R_{\rm phys}$ shrinks, driving the geometric saturation ratio $S \equiv R_T/R_{\rm phys}$ to large values across the degenerate stellar sequence.

For Sirius B ($M \approx 1.02 M_{\odot}$, $\rho \approx 2.4 \times 10^6$ g/cm³):

\begin{equation} \label{eq:jupiter_radius} R_{\rm phys} \approx 5{,}800 \text{ km} \quad \text{(observed)} \end{equation}
\begin{equation} \label{eq:jupiter_soliton} R_T \approx 289{,}000 \text{ km} \quad \text{(TEP saturation scale)} \end{equation}
\begin{equation} \label{eq:jupiter_screening} \text{Geometric Saturation Ratio } S = \frac{R_T}{R_{\rm phys}} \approx 50\times. \end{equation}

With a mean density \(\rho \approx 2.4 \times 10^6\) g/cm³ far exceeding the reference scale \(\rho_T \approx 20\) g/cm³, the geometric saturation ratio \(S = R_T/R_{\rm phys} \approx 50\) places Sirius B deep within the compact-body regime (\(R_T \gg R_{\rm phys}\)). This is consistent with the expected nonlinear screening hierarchy; the quantitative magnitude of physical scalar-gradient suppression in binary orbital dynamics is determined by the corresponding nonlinear kinetic and shell-impedance field solution (Paper 0 §7.3), rather than by the geometric ratio \(S\) alone.

4.2 The Empirical Screening Law

Extending this analysis across 26 astrophysical objects (planets, brown dwarfs, main sequence stars, white dwarfs, neutron stars, and binary pulsars) yields a compact summary of how the defined screening factor varies with density. Restricting the regression fit to the 11 dense objects ($\rho > \rho_T$, excluding planets and black holes) gives:

\begin{equation} \label{eq:screening_law} S \propto \rho^{\beta_{\rm scr}}, \qquad \beta_{\rm scr} = 0.334 \quad (R^2 = 0.99995). \end{equation}

The exponent $\beta_{\rm scr} = 0.334$ is statistically indistinguishable from 1/3. This density-dependent screening law is a projection of the source-charge screening operator $\mathcal{S}_\Sigma(\mathcal{E})$ (Paper 0 §7) onto the UCD density regime; it is not an independent screening operator. Two sector assignments are stated explicitly. The geometric diagnostic $S = R_T/R_{\rm phys}$ tracks the shear-sector $\mathcal{S}_\Sigma$ regime — density is the licensed stand-in for the ambient-steepness criterion inside dense macroscopic matter (Paper 0 §8) — while the scale $\rho_T$ that sets $R_T$ is itself calibrated through the clock-amplitude projection $S_A = \min[1,(\bar\rho/\rho_T)^{1/3}]$ (Paper 0 §3). Because each object's density is evaluated at its physical radius, $\bar\rho = 3M/(4\pi R_{\rm phys}^3)$, the diagnostic carries an enclosed-acceleration equivalent: the criterion $S\sim1$ corresponds to $GM/R_{\rm phys}^2 \propto \bar\rho\,R_{\rm phys}$, so the density and acceleration readings of the hierarchy agree object-by-object. Under the stated definitions, this is the expected scaling: if $R_T \propto M^{1/3}$ and $R_{phys} \propto (M/\rho)^{1/3}$, then:

\begin{equation} \label{eq:screening_derivation} S = \frac{R_T}{R_{phys}} = \frac{M^{1/3}}{M^{1/3}/\rho^{1/3}} = \rho^{1/3}. \end{equation}

Interpretation of the Screening Law

It is important to recognize that the regression $S \propto \rho^{0.334}$ is primarily a consistency check, not independent evidence for the scaling law. Since $S \equiv R_T/R_{\text{phys}}$ and the radii are defined by mass-density relations, the slope $\sim 1/3$ is algebraically expected given the model assumptions.

The value of this analysis is not to prove the scaling 'ab initio', but to demonstrate that a single saturation scale $\rho_T$ yields a consistent screening hierarchy across 15 orders of magnitude in density ($R^2 \approx 1$) without requiring regime-dependent adjustments. The high $R^2$ confirms internal consistency; it does not constitute independent confirmation of TEP.

Furthermore, the extreme screening factors observed in binary pulsars ($S \sim 29{,}000$) are consistent with a strongly non-linear suppression mechanism in the scalar sector. The empirical screening hierarchy ($S$ vs $\rho$) derives from the canonical Temporal Topology mechanism (Paper 0), where screening operates via the continuous spatial profile of the scalar field governed by non-linear superposition of field gradients (Temporal Shear). Box 6.5 (Section 6) sketches a candidate route from the canonical action to soliton-like saturation, showing how the interplay between the kinetic term and a candidate saturation potential could generate the characteristic $M^{1/3}$ scaling and screening behavior. The detailed dynamical derivation is not required for the empirical hierarchy used here.

The empirical screening law is a direct consequence of the $M^{1/3}$ soliton scaling, providing cross-regime consistency under a single $\rho_T$ rather than independent confirmation (see Note on Interpretation above).

4.7 Planetary Rotational Kinematics and the Wesson–MacDonald Angular Momentum Laws

An independent dynamical test of the saturation radius $R_T(M)$ and screening factor $S = R_T(M)/R_{\rm phys}$ is provided by the rotational kinematics of Solar System bodies. In Newtonian gravity, planetary rotation periods ($T_{\rm rot} \approx 9.9\text{ hr}$ for Jupiter to $24.6\text{ hr}$ for Mars among unbraked planets, and $58.6\text{--}243\text{ d}$ for solar-tide-braked Mercury and Venus) and equatorial linear speeds ($v_{\rm eq} \approx 0.24\text{ km s}^{-1}$ for Mars to $12.57\text{ km s}^{-1}$ for Jupiter) appear unorganized by radius or heliocentric distance. Yet two empirical regularities have long been noted in the astrophysical literature without a first-principles gravitational derivation: the MacDonald–Brosche specific angular momentum scaling $j \equiv J/M \propto M^{2/3}\text{--}M^{0.8}$ across minor and major planets (Brosche 1963; MacDonald 1964), and Wesson's universal angular-momentum relation $J = p\,M^2$ with empirical constant $p_{\rm obs} \approx 10^{-15}\text{ cm}^2\text{ g}^{-1}\text{ s}^{-1}$ spanning planetary systems to spiral galaxies (Wesson 1979, 1981)—a value approximately $680$ times larger than the General Relativistic Kerr scale $G/c = 2.23 \times 10^{-18}\text{ cm}^2\text{ g}^{-1}\text{ s}^{-1}$.

Both regularities follow directly from the Temporal Topology saturation scale $\rho_T = 20\text{ g/cm}^3$ and the nested temporal hierarchy of Paper 0 (§7). First, because $R_T(M)^3 = 3M/(4\pi\rho_T)$ is proportional to mass, the characteristic dynamical frequency at the saturation boundary $R_T(M)$ is strictly mass-independent:

\begin{equation} \label{eq:omega_T_universal} \omega_T \equiv \sqrt{\frac{GM}{R_T(M)^3}} = \sqrt{\frac{4\pi G\rho_T}{3}} = 2.365 \times 10^{-3}\text{ rad s}^{-1} \qquad \left(\tau_T = \frac{2\pi}{\omega_T} = 0.738\text{ hr} = 44.3\text{ min}\right). \end{equation}

At the saturation radius $R_T(M)$, the dimensionless proper-time lapse depth is $u_T(M) \equiv GM/(R_T(M)c^2) = \omega_T^2 R_T(M)^2/c^2 \propto M^{2/3}$. Consequently, the natural specific angular-momentum capacity of the saturation core rotating at $\omega_T$ is directly proportional to its proper-time depth:

\begin{equation} \label{eq:j_T_capacity} j_T(M) \equiv R_T(M)^2\,\omega_T = \frac{c^2}{\omega_T}\,u_T(M) \propto M^{2/3}, \end{equation}

with universal proportionality constant $c^2/\omega_T = 3.801 \times 10^{19}\text{ m}^2\text{ s}^{-1}$ per unit fractional proper-time dilation. Second, in the nested environmental hierarchy of Paper 0 (§7), Solar System bodies condense inside the ambient Solar-Circle Galactic temporal well of depth $u_{\rm gal} = (v_{\rm gal}/c)^2 = 5.385 \times 10^{-7}$ ($v_{\rm gal} = 220\text{ km s}^{-1}$, $c/v_{\rm gal} = 1/\sqrt{u_{\rm gal}} = 1362.7$; adopting modern Gaia/VLBI solar-circle estimates $v_{\rm gal} \approx 229\text{--}236\text{ km s}^{-1}$ shifts predictions by $4\text{--}7\%$). As an action-motivated, zero-refit rotational scaling proposal, equating the mixed time–space kinematic transport coupling $(v_{\rm eq}/c)\sqrt{u_{\rm gal}}$ — equivalently, the advective velocity cross-term $(v_{\rm eq}\,v_{\rm gal})/c^2$ between internal circulation and Galactic drift — across the saturation boundary to the local proper-time depth $u_T(M)$ yields the equatorial velocity relation for ambient-coupled planets:

\begin{equation} \label{eq:veq_tep_law} \frac{v_{\rm eq}^{\rm TEP}}{c} = \frac{u_T(M)}{\sqrt{u_{\rm gal}}} \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad v_{\rm eq}^{\rm TEP} = \frac{GM}{R_T(M)\,v_{\rm gal}} = \frac{v_T(M)^2}{v_{\rm gal}}, \end{equation}

where $v_T(M) = \sqrt{GM/R_T(M)} = R_T(M)\omega_T$ is the circular velocity at the TEP saturation radius. Across Earth, Uranus, Neptune, and Saturn, the observed dimensionless ratio $(v_{\rm eq}/c)/u_T$ averages $1384.8 \pm 99.6$, matching the Galactic constant $c/v_{\rm gal} = 1362.7$ within $+1.6\%$ ($v_{\rm obs}/v_{\rm TEP} = 1.016 \pm 0.073$; Table 4.3, Figure 6) with zero fitted parameters. Equation (\ref{eq:veq_tep_law}) predicts rotation periods of $25.48\text{ hr}$ for Earth (observed $23.93\text{ hr}$), $17.14\text{ hr}$ for Uranus (observed $17.25\text{ hr}$; Lamy et al. 2025), $14.87\text{ hr}$ for Neptune (observed $16.11\text{ hr}$), and $11.55\text{ hr}$ for Saturn (observed $10.66\text{ hr}$). Moreover, the departures of Jupiter ($v_{\rm obs}/v_{\rm TEP} = 0.62$) and Mars ($v_{\rm obs}/v_{\rm TEP} = 2.44$) from the single-branch ambient formula Eq. ($\ref{eq:veq_tep_law}$) reflect the two-branch angular-momentum geometry of Figure 6b (Step 15): because the ambient Galactic Wesson branch $j_{\rm TEP}(M) = GM/(2v_{\rm gal}) \propto M^1$ grows faster with mass than the small-body soliton angular-momentum capacity $j_{\rm sol}(M) = \eta_0 R_T(M)^2\omega_T \propto M^{2/3}$ (where $\eta_0 = 0.129$ is a single empirical efficiency parameter calibrated from the small-body median, close to the classical secular bar-mode / rotational-shedding stability fraction $\eta_{\rm sec} = k_{\rm core}(\omega_{\max}/\omega_T) = \tfrac{1}{2} \times \tfrac{1}{4} = \tfrac{1}{8} = 0.125$ at the $S \to 1$ core boundary), their ratio $$\frac{j_{\rm TEP}(M)}{j_{\rm sol}(M)} = \frac{R_T(M)\,\omega_T}{2\,\eta_0\,v_{\rm gal}} = \left(\frac{M}{M_{\rm cap}}\right)^{1/3}$$ crosses unity at a unique crossover mass $M_{\rm cap} = \frac{4\pi}{3}\rho_T(2\eta_0 v_{\rm gal}/\omega_T)^3 = 1.16 \times 10^{27}\text{ kg} = 194.2\,M_\oplus = 0.611\,M_{\rm Jup}$ ($R_T(M_{\rm cap}) = 24{,}013\text{ km}$; intersection of the solid and dashed curves in Figure 6b). For Earth ($1.0\,M_\oplus$), Uranus ($14.5\,M_\oplus$), Neptune ($17.1\,M_\oplus$), and Saturn ($95.2\,M_\oplus < M_{\rm cap}$), $j_{\rm TEP}(M)/j_{\rm sol}(M) \in [0.173,\,0.788] < 1$, so all four lie on the unsaturated ambient branch ($v_{\rm eq}^{\rm 2br} = v_{\rm eq}^{\rm TEP}$, with Saturn at $9.87\text{ km s}^{-1}$ vs $9.11\text{ km s}^{-1}$, $+8.4\%$). Conversely, Jupiter ($317.8\,M_\oplus > M_{\rm cap}$, $j_{\rm TEP}/j_{\rm sol} = 1.178 > 1$) is the sole Solar System planet above $M_{\rm cap}$, so its specific angular momentum saturates at $j_{\rm sol}(M_{\rm Jup})$ ($\eta_{\rm obs} = 0.125$ vs $\eta_0 = 0.129$), yielding $v_{\rm eq}^{\rm 2br} = j_{\rm sol}(M_{\rm Jup})/(k_{\rm MOI}R_{\rm Jup}) = 12.95\text{ km s}^{-1}$ ($T_{\rm 2br} = 9.64\text{ hr}$), within $-2.9\%$ of Jupiter's observed $12.57\text{ km s}^{-1}$ ($T_{\rm obs} = 9.93\text{ hr}$, $v_{\rm obs}/v_{\rm 2br} = 0.971$; across all five major Solar System planets, $v_{\rm obs}/v_{\rm 2br} = 1.007 \pm 0.066$, and $1.008 \pm 0.060$ across all nine planetary and substellar speed-limit systems, Figure 6a, diamond markers). \begin{equation} \label{eq:kmoi_screening} k_{\rm MOI} \simeq \frac{1}{2}\,S = \frac{1}{2}\,\frac{R_T(M)}{R_{\rm phys}} \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad I \simeq \frac{1}{2}\,M\,R_T(M)\,R_{\rm phys}, \end{equation}

with $2k_{\rm MOI}/S = 1.039 \pm 0.027$ across the three iron-core terrestrial planets ($1.017$ for Earth, $1.031$ for Venus, and $1.069$ for Mercury; because these three bodies span a narrow bulk density range $\bar\rho = 5.24\text{--}5.51\text{ g/cm}^3$, their agreement serves as a single-regime terrestrial amplitude check rather than a lever-arm slope test) and $2k_{\rm MOI}/S = 1.169 \pm 0.142$ across all eight planets spanning an $8\times$ range in mean density ($\bar\rho = 0.69\text{--}5.51\text{ g/cm}^3$: $1.112$ for Neptune, $1.137$ for Uranus, $1.261$ for low-pressure Mars near the incompressible uniform-sphere ceiling $k_{\rm MOI} \to 2/5 = 0.4$, and $1.334$ for Jupiter and $1.389$ for Saturn where compressible H/He envelopes inflate $R_{\rm eq}$). \begin{equation} \label{eq:wesson_tep_law} j_{\rm TEP}(M) = k_{\rm MOI}\,R_{\rm phys}\,v_{\rm eq}^{\rm TEP} = \frac{GM}{2\,v_{\rm gal}}, \qquad J_{\rm TEP}(M) = p_{\rm TEP}\,M^2, \qquad p_{\rm TEP} \equiv \frac{G}{2\,v_{\rm gal}} = 1.517 \times 10^{-15}\text{ cm}^2\text{ g}^{-1}\text{ s}^{-1}, \end{equation}

corresponding to a dimensionless Kerr-like spin parameter $\chi_{\rm TEP} \equiv cJ/(GM^2) = c/(2v_{\rm gal}) = 1/(2\sqrt{u_{\rm gal}}) = 681.3$. Crucially, for any body, the exact algebraic identity $$\frac{J_{\rm obs}}{J_{\rm TEP}} \equiv \left(\frac{2\,k_{\rm MOI}}{S}\right) \left(\frac{v_{\rm eq}^{\rm obs}}{v_{\rm eq}^{\rm TEP}}\right)$$ holds by definition: although $\rho_T$ cancels out of the product $J_{\rm TEP}(M)$, measuring $k_{\rm MOI}$ and $v_{\rm eq}$ separately constrains $\rho_T \approx 20\text{ g/cm}^3$ through both factors simultaneously ($2k_{\rm MOI}/S \propto \rho_T^{1/3}$ and $v_{\rm obs}/v_{\rm TEP} \propto \rho_T^{-1/3}$—an unadjusted $\rho_T \sim 220\text{ g/cm}^3$ would shift both factors by $2.23\times$ in opposite directions, to $2.61$ and $0.45$). Inverting Equation (\ref{eq:veq_tep_law}) directly via $\rho_T^{\rm spin} = \frac{3}{4\pi}(v_{\rm eq}^{\rm obs}v_{\rm gal}/GM^{2/3})^3 = \rho_T(v_{\rm obs}/v_{\rm TEP})^3$ across the four unsaturated ambient-coupled planets yields individual spin-implied saturation densities of $24.1\text{ g/cm}^3$ (Earth), $19.6\text{ g/cm}^3$ (Uranus, using the 2025 HST auroral rotation period $17\text{ h }14\text{ m }52\text{ s} = 17.247864 \pm 0.000010\text{ hr}$, Lamy et al. 2025), $15.7\text{ g/cm}^3$ (Neptune), and $25.5\text{ g/cm}^3$ (Saturn), with a geometric mean of $\rho_T^{\rm spin} = 20.9\text{ g/cm}^3$ ($21.2 \pm 4.4\text{ g/cm}^3$ arithmetic mean; and $21.1\text{ g/cm}^3$ geometric mean across the nine planetary and substellar systems under the one-parameter $\eta_0 = 0.129$ two-branch model), while inverting Equation (\ref{eq:kmoi_screening}) via $\rho_T^{\rm MOI} = \bar\rho/(8k_{\rm MOI}^3)$ yields $\rho_T^{\rm MOI,\text{terr}} = 17.9 \pm 1.4\text{ g/cm}^3$ sample scatter across the three iron-core terrestrial planets ($19.1\text{ g/cm}^3$ for Earth, $18.2\text{ g/cm}^3$ for Venus with $\pm 20\%$ propagated $k_{\rm MOI}$ uncertainty, $16.4\text{ g/cm}^3$ for Mercury) and $\rho_T^{\rm MOI} = 13.7 \pm 4.2\text{ g/cm}^3$ across all eight planets, exhibiting a systematic trend with planet composition from iron-core terrestrials ($16.4\text{--}19.1\text{ g/cm}^3$) to ice giants ($14.8\text{ g/cm}^3$ for Neptune, $13.9\text{ g/cm}^3$ for Uranus), low-pressure Mars ($10.0\text{ g/cm}^3$), and compressible H/He gas giants ($9.0\text{ g/cm}^3$ for Jupiter, $8.3\text{ g/cm}^3$ for Saturn). Across the five ambient-coupled major planets (Earth, Uranus, Neptune, Saturn, and Jupiter), the observed spin angular momenta match the zero-parameter Wesson relation $p_{\rm TEP} = G/(2v_{\rm gal})$ at $J_{\rm obs}/J_{\rm TEP} = 1.11 \pm 0.25$ ($\bar\chi_{\rm obs} = 758.7 \pm 169.0$ versus $\chi_{\rm TEP} = 681.3$).

A necessary condition separating the ambient Galactic Wesson branch ($J \propto M^2$) from the low-mass intrinsic soliton-clock branch ($j \propto M^{2/3}$) is obtained by comparing each body's two-branch screening shell radius $r_*(M) = \sqrt{GM/g_t}$ ($g_t = cH_0/2 = 3.40 \times 10^{-10}\text{ m s}^{-2}$; Paper 0 §7) to its heliocentric orbital semimajor axis $a$ (Table 4.3). Unbraked planets whose screening shell reaches their orbit ($r_*(M)/a \gtrsim 1$, with Neptune at the boundary) can couple to the Galactic shear floor: Jupiter ($r_*/a = 24.79$, $J_{\rm obs}/J_{\rm TEP} = 0.824$), Saturn ($r_*/a = 7.37$, $J_{\rm obs}/J_{\rm TEP} = 1.505$), Earth ($r_*/a = 7.24$, $J_{\rm obs}/J_{\rm TEP} = 1.083$), Uranus ($r_*/a = 1.44$, $J_{\rm obs}/J_{\rm TEP} = 1.129$), and Neptune ($r_*(M_{\rm Nep}) = 29.97\text{ AU}$ versus $a_{\rm Nep} = 30.11\text{ AU}$, $r_*/a = 0.995$, $J_{\rm obs}/J_{\rm TEP} = 1.026$), whereas Mars ($r_*/a = 1.56$, $\chi_{\rm obs} = 2094.6$, $\eta_{\rm rot} = 0.033$) sits in the transition zone between the two branches. Conversely, for small bodies whose screening shell is much smaller than their orbital radius ($r_*(M)/a \ll 1$: Vesta $0.020$, Ceres $0.033$, Haumea $0.004$), rotation decouples from the Galactic floor and follows the intrinsic soliton-clock capacity $j_{\rm sol}(M) = \eta_{\rm rot}\,R_T(M)^2\,\omega_T \propto M^{2/3}$ with mean efficiency $\eta_{\rm rot} \equiv j_{\rm obs}/j_T = 0.127 \pm 0.080$ (median $0.125$; $0.129$ for Ceres), yielding a log-log slope of $0.941 \pm 0.059$ ($R^2 = 0.9735$, $p = 8.85 \times 10^{-7}$) against $u_T(M)$ across seven orders of magnitude in mass (Figure 6). Notably, the combined Earth+Moon system ($r_*/a = 7.28$, $\eta_{\rm rot} = 0.119$) sits near the soliton capacity branch ($\eta_{\rm rot} \approx 0.13$) following the Moon-forming giant impact, while subsequent and still-ongoing lunar tidal dissipation ($\dot{a}_{\rm Moon} \approx +3.8\text{ cm yr}^{-1}$, lengthening the day by $\sim 2\text{ ms century}^{-1}$) has spun Earth down across the intermediate region to its present-day epochal value near the Wesson line ($\chi_{\rm obs} = 737.8$ vs. $\chi_{\rm TEP} = 681.3$, a secular transit rather than a static tidal equilibrium). Extending this soliton-capacity branch out of sample across the expanded minor-planet/dwarf-planet sequence (Hygiea, Pallas, Vesta, Ceres, Quaoar, and Haumea; $\eta_{\rm rot}$ median $0.138$, mean $0.157 \pm 0.076$) and beyond the Solar System to super-Jupiters and ultra-cool brown dwarfs ($M > M_{\rm cap} = 0.61\,M_{\rm Jup}$; Snellen et al. 2014; Gagné et al. 2017; Bryan et al. 2020; Tannock et al. 2021) exposes a direct out-of-sample test of both $R_T(M)$ and the clock-amplitude saturation ceiling $S_A(\bar\rho) = \min[1,\,(\bar\rho/\rho_T)^{1/3}] = \min[1,\,S]$ (Paper 0 §3). Because the active saturation core inside a body has radius $R_{\rm active} = S_A\,R_{\rm phys} = \min[R_T(M),\,R_{\rm phys}]$, the maximum specific angular momentum is $j_{\rm cap}(M,\,R_{\rm phys}) = \eta_0\,\min[R_T(M),\,R_{\rm phys}]^2\,\omega_T$, which defines two distinct high-mass regimes above $M_{\rm cap}$: (i) for unscreened planetary-mass objects with $\bar\rho < \rho_T = 20\text{ g/cm}^3$ ($S < 1$, $M \lesssim 16\,M_{\rm Jup}$), $R_{\rm active} = R_T(M) < R_{\rm phys}$ and the rotational ceiling rises as $v_{\rm cap}(M) = \eta_0 R_T(M)^2\omega_T/(k_{\rm MOI}R_{\rm phys})$ — predicting $v_{\rm cap} = 64.9\text{ km s}^{-1}$ ($P_{\rm cap} = 2.35\text{ hr}$) for the fastest-rotating planetary-mass object known, SIMP J013656.5+093347 ($M = 12.7 \pm 1.0\,M_{\rm Jup}, R = 1.22\,R_{\rm Jup}, \bar\rho = 8.67\text{ g/cm}^3, S = 0.76$; Gagné et al. 2017), matching its observed $v_{\rm eq} = 63.1\text{ km s}^{-1}$ ($P_{\rm obs} = 2.414\text{ hr}$, $v_{\rm obs}/v_{\rm cap} = 0.972$, within $-2.8\%$), while young contracting exoplanets such as $\beta$ Pictoris b ($11\,M_{\rm Jup}, v\sin i = 25\text{ km s}^{-1}$, deprojected $v_{\rm eq} \approx 31.8\text{ km s}^{-1}$ vs. $v_{\rm cap} = 46.6\text{ km s}^{-1}$; Snellen et al. 2014) and the 27-object sample of Bryan et al. (2020; $v/v_{\rm breakup} \in [0.05,\,0.31]$, $0/27$ exceeding the $v_{\rm eq}/v_{\rm breakup} \le 0.45$ ceiling) lie below this upper boundary; and (ii) once $\bar\rho \ge \rho_T = 20\text{ g/cm}^3$ ($S = R_T/R_{\rm phys} \ge 1$, in degenerate brown dwarfs with $M \gtrsim 16\,M_{\rm Jup}$), the active radius saturates at the physical surface ($R_{\rm active} = R_{\rm phys}$), so $R_{\rm phys}$ cancels out of $P_{\min} = 2\pi R_{\rm phys}/v_{\rm cap}$ and locks the minimum rotation period of all degenerate brown dwarfs onto a universal, mass- and radius-independent floor set directly by $\tau_T = 2\pi/\omega_T = 0.7381\text{ hr}$ ($44.3\text{ min}$): $$P_{\min}^{\rm TEP}(\bar\rho \ge \rho_T) = \frac{k_{\rm MOI}}{\eta_0}\,\tau_T = \frac{0.205}{0.129}\,\tau_T = 1.172\text{ hr} \quad (70.3\text{ min}),$$ where $k_{\rm MOI} = 0.205$ is the exact Lane–Emden gyration factor of an $n = 1.5$ degenerate polytrope. With $\eta_0 = 0.129$ fixed by the small-body median, this zero-refit floor reproduces the empirical rotational speed limit of ultra-cool brown dwarfs discovered by Tannock et al. (2021): across the three fastest-rotating brown dwarfs known — 2MASS J0348$-$6022 ($43\,M_{\rm Jup}, \bar\rho = 70.8\text{ g/cm}^3, S = 1.52, P_{\rm obs} = 1.080\text{ hr}$), 2MASS J1219$+$3128 ($49\,M_{\rm Jup}, \bar\rho = 66.6\text{ g/cm}^3, S = 1.49, P_{\rm obs} = 1.140\text{ hr}$), and 2MASS J0407$+$1546 ($67\,M_{\rm Jup}, \bar\rho = 156.4\text{ g/cm}^3, S = 1.98, P_{\rm obs} = 1.230\text{ hr}$) — the observed period floor is $1.150 \pm 0.075\text{ hr}$ (matching $P_{\min}^{\rm TEP} = 1.172\text{ hr}$ within $+1.9\%$, whereas Newtonian Keplerian breakup sits at $P_{\rm breakup} = 0.28\text{--}0.43\text{ hr}$; evaluating the same law at the parameter-free secular stability fraction $\eta_{\rm sec} = \tfrac{1}{8} = 0.125$ with zero small-body calibration predicts $v_{\rm eq}^{\rm th} = 12.54\text{ km s}^{-1}$ for Jupiter [$-0.2\%$ vs $12.57\text{ km s}^{-1}$], $62.9\text{ km s}^{-1}$ for SIMP J0136 [$-0.3\%$ vs $63.1\text{ km s}^{-1}$], and $P_{\min}^{\rm th} = 8k_{\rm MOI}\tau_T = 1.210\text{ hr}$ [$0.8\sigma$ from $1.150 \pm 0.075\text{ hr}$]). Across all nine major planetary and substellar speed-limit systems spanning $1\,M_\oplus$ to $67\,M_{\rm Jup}$ ($21{,}300\,M_\oplus$; Earth, Uranus, Neptune, Saturn, Jupiter, SIMP J0136, and the three Tannock et al. brown dwarfs), the two-branch $+\;S_A$ saturation law achieves $v_{\rm obs}/v_{\rm 2br} = 1.008 \pm 0.060$ ($\rho_T^{\rm rot} = 21.1\text{ g/cm}^3$ geometric mean; Figure 6).

Box 4.1: Summary — Four Cross-Regime Consistency Tests

The screening analysis provides four cross-regime consistency tests of the TEP framework under a single $\rho_T$:

  1. The RBH-1 Crossover: The saturation radius is consistent with the Schwarzschild radius for RBH-1 ($M \approx 2 \times 10^7 M_{\odot}$, near the exact crossover mass $M_\times = \sqrt{3c^6/(32\pi G^3\rho_T)} \approx 3.04 \times 10^7 M_{\odot}$) within combined uncertainties ($R_T/R_S \approx 1.3$). This provides a tightly constrained cross-regime consistency check under the fixed $\rho_T$ calibration.
  2. The Screening Exponent: The empirical scaling $S \propto \rho^{\beta_{\rm scr}}$ with $\beta_{\rm scr} = 0.334$ is fitted to the 11 dense objects ($\rho > \rho_T$) spanning ~12 orders of magnitude in density, with $R^2 = 0.99995$. The exponent 1/3 is a direct consequence of the $M^{1/3}$ saturation scaling. The high $R^2$ partly reflects the definitional structure of $S$ (the $\rho^{1/3}$ dependence is algebraically favoured once $R_T(M)$ enters the construction); the substantive content is the absence of large deviations, not the correlation coefficient — see Appendix B.4.
  3. GR Test Consistency: All five precision tests of General Relativity are consistent with screening factors that suppress scalar contributions below current measurement limits.
  4. Planetary Spin and Wesson Constant: Combining $\rho_T = 20$ g/cm³ with the Galactic temporal depth $u_{\rm gal} = (v_{\rm gal}/c)^2$ yields the zero-parameter ambient equatorial speed relation ($v_{\rm eq} = c\,u_T/\sqrt{u_{\rm gal}}$, mean ratio $1.016 \pm 0.073$ and $\rho_T^{\rm spin} = 20.9$ g/cm³ across Earth, Uranus, Neptune, and Saturn) and the zero-parameter Wesson constant $p_{\rm TEP} = G/(2v_{\rm gal}) = 1.52 \times 10^{-15}$ cm$^2$ g$^{-1}$ s$^{-1}$, while the conditional one-parameter ($\eta_0 = 0.129$) two-branch $+\;S_A$ extension matches $N = 9$ planetary and substellar speed-limit systems from Earth to $67\,M_{\rm Jup}$ at $v_{\rm obs}/v_{\rm 2br} = 1.008 \pm 0.060$ ($\rho_T^{\rm rot} = 21.1$ g/cm³, including SIMP J0136 and the $1.172$ hr brown-dwarf period floor) alongside cold-planet moments of inertia ($2k_{\rm MOI}/S = 1.039 \pm 0.027$ across the three iron-core terrestrial planets and $1.17 \pm 0.14$ across all eight planets).

The TEP framework does not violate established physics. It extends the metric structure to include a conformal sector that becomes observable only in specific density regimes—such as those probed by GNSS atomic clocks and cosmological soliton-scale systems.
